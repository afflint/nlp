The exam at a glance

A single oral interview, made of two parts.

Part 1

Theory questions on the topics of the course

  • They cover the whole programme: lectures, slides and the material indicated by the instructors.
  • Not only the topics related to your project: prepare for them independently of the project work.
Part 2

Discussion of your final project

  • The research paper, the code repository and the presentation.
  • Assessed on the output and on your ability to explain and justify every choice, including the content of your reference paper and why your work goes beyond it.

The theory questions are mandatory and indispensable to pass the exam. A satisfactory answer is a necessary condition for a passing grade. An excellent project does not compensate for insufficient knowledge of the course contents, and a good theory preparation does not replace the project: the final grade takes both into account.

The final project

A short study on one topic of the course, with a precise research question and measurable objectives.

1
Research question
Precise, with measurable objectives.
2
Methodology
A proposed way of solving the research question.
3
Experimental verification
The results obtained, evaluated with explicit metrics.
4
Critical discussion
The emphasis is not on obtaining high performance, but on discussing the results to understand the potential effectiveness of the methodology.
What is new this year

Ten tracks, and you design your own project

The idea

No ready-made project descriptions.

  • We propose ten broad themes. You choose one and design your own project within it.
  • The tracks describe a scope and the requirements that a good project must meet. They are not assignments: the research question, the data, the models, the metrics and the experimental design are yours.
  • Projects that are very similar to each other, or that merely apply a generic recipe to a topic, will be harder to defend at the interview.
Five steps
  1. Choose a track One of the ten themes
  2. Choose a reference paper From the closed list of venues: study it carefully, it is your starting point
  3. Formulate your own research question With measurable objectives, going beyond the reference paper
  4. Design and carry out the experiments Within the resources you have
  5. Write the paper, prepare the repository and the presentation LaTeX paper, GitHub repository, 10-minute talk
Reference paper

Anchor your project to at least one reference paper, from a closed list of venues.

NLP and computational linguistics
  • ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, EACL, AACL-IJCNLP, COLING, LREC-COLING, CoNLL, *SEM
  • TACL and Computational Linguistics
  • Papers in the Findings of ACL, EMNLP, NAACL and EACL are also accepted
Machine learning and AI
  • NeurIPS (including the Datasets & Benchmarks track), ICML, ICLR, COLM, AAAI, IJCAI
  • TMLR, JAIR, JMLR

The paper is a source of inspiration: you are not expected to reproduce it, but you must be able to present it, explain its methodology and results, and discuss its limitations. Your own question and experiments go beyond it, and the report makes clear what is yours and what comes from the reference. Any other source is welcome: cite everything you use.

What you deliver

A paper, a repository and a presentation.

The paper

A short article, 4 to 8 pages

  • Written in LaTeX.
  • Not less than 4 pages and no more than 8.
  • Four sections (next slides).
The repository

Code and reproducible results on GitHub

  • Access to a GitHub repository with the code.
  • Reproducible experimental results.
The presentation

10 minutes, in English, with slides

  • It covers your reference paper as well as your own work.
  • The project is discussed after the presentation.
Structure of the paper

Four sections

What the paper must show

What is yours and what comes from the reference.

  • The report makes explicit what is original with respect to the reference paper.
  • Results are presented as plots and/or tables, and discussed critically: their limits, and ideas for future work.
The sections
  1. Introduction Overview of the project, short discussion of the pertinent literature, the reference paper and how your work relates to it
  2. Research question and methodology Clear statement of the goals, overview of the approach, formal definition of the problem; what is original
  3. Experimental results The dataset, the metrics and the experimental methodology; results as plots and/or tables
  4. Concluding remarks Critical discussion of the results, their limits, and ideas for future work
Procedure

How to book the interview

The rule

No preliminary approval of the project.

  • You choose a track and proceed on your own.
  • Exam dates are just for the registration of the final grade. The interview (theory questions and project discussion) is set by appointment.
  • How to contact Prof. Ferrara and submit the project is described in the Ariel web site (forum thread of the course).
Three steps
  1. Subscribe to any available date It only registers the final grade
  2. Contact Prof. Ferrara As soon as the project is finished and ready to be discussed, and after the date of your subscription has expired
  3. Set up an appointment and discuss your work Theory questions and project discussion, in a single interview
Resources and feasibility

Projects must be feasible with limited computational resources.

What is realistic
  • A laptop and free-tier notebooks.
  • Small open models.
  • Subsets of datasets.
  • Inexpensive API usage.
What we ask of you
  • You decide how ambitious to be.
  • Justify your choices: for example, why a given model size or sample size is sufficient to answer your research question.
  • Every track says where the cost grows.

A small, well-designed and carefully analysed experiment is preferable to a large, poorly controlled one.

Using AI

Generative AI may be used, as an object of investigation and as a tool.

Encouraged
  • Explore how the models function and interact with them creatively.
  • Use them as inspiration or assistance in ideation, drafting or experimentation.
Not allowed
  • Using AI as a substitute for original work: the structure, the reasoning and the understanding of the project remain yours.
Mandatory, if AI was used at any stage

A disclaimer that specifies

  • Which models have been used.
  • For what purposes: drafting text, summarizing ideas, generating code or examples.
  • To what extent the outputs were modified, verified or integrated into the final submission.

The interview evaluates the depth of your understanding. Any lack of clarity, or over-reliance on AI-generated material without proper insight, may negatively affect the evaluation. The theory questions test what you have learned: they cannot be prepared with AI assistance during the exam.

Instructions on coding

Not a single large notebook: a structured, maintainable repository.

Structure
  • Python modules and packages.
  • Object-oriented programming where appropriate: classes for models, datasets, evaluation pipelines.
  • Separated layers: data loading and preprocessing, model interface, evaluation, visualization.
Notebooks
  • For demonstration, experimentation and visualization.
  • Not for hosting the full application logic.
  • Each one imports and showcases components of the main modules.
Quality
  • Meaningful docstrings, comments and clear function signatures.
  • A repository that is clean, reproducible and extensible: others can replicate the results or build upon them.

Track 1
Strategy and Negotiation
Game theory and emergent behaviour in LLMs

What strategies emerge when language models play formally defined games, and how do they compare with theory and with human behaviour?

Possible perspectives
  • Repeated games: do models cooperate, retaliate, forgive?
  • Bargaining and fairness: ultimatum, dictator, public goods
  • Information and deception: private information, cheap talk
  • Sensitivity to the setting: framing, persona, rounds, model size
  • Adaptation: to the opponent’s strategy, or a fixed disposition?
  • Populations and tournaments: scripted strategies, round-robin
A good project has
  • A formalised game: players, actions, information, payoffs
  • A point of comparison: a theoretical prediction or human data
  • Strategies at the centre: what emerges, how stable it is
  • Experimental rigour: many repetitions, seeds, statistical tests
Common pitfalls
  • Chat models persuading each other, with no payoff and no formal game
  • Only win rates, without the strategies behind them
  • Strategies labelled by impression, not by a checkable criterion
Search keywordsrepeated games, iterated prisoner’s dilemma, ultimatum game, bargaining, negotiation, Nash equilibrium, cheap talk, strategic reasoning, behavioural game theory, theory of mind, LLM agents, multi-agent systems, cooperation.
Feasibility and resourcesDiscrete actions and short horizons are cheap, with small open models or low-cost APIs. The cost grows with rounds, agents and repetitions: justify the sample size.
Track 2
Fact-Checking and Retrieval
Lexical vs semantic search with LLMs

How far can we trust what is retrieved, and what changes between lexical and embedding-based retrieval when the goal is to verify a claim?

Possible perspectives
  • Lexical, dense or hybrid: which claims does each approach win?
  • Where the error comes from: retrieval or verification?
  • Robustness: contradictory, outdated, poisoned evidence; domain, language
  • Evidence vs model knowledge: what if they conflict?
  • Hard claims: negations, numbers, time, multi-hop, insufficient evidence
  • Domains: health, climate, economy, science
A good project has
  • Retrieval evaluated separately: recall@k, nDCG apart from the verdict metrics
  • A serious lexical baseline: well configured, not a strawman
  • Error analysis at the centre: categorise the failures
  • At least one stress condition: poisoned corpus, domain or language shift, time
Common pitfalls
  • Only end-to-end accuracy, concluding “dense is better” without why
  • A tuned dense model against an untuned lexical baseline
  • An LLM as the only judge of evidence, unchecked
Search keywordsfact-checking, claim verification, evidence retrieval, dense retrieval, BM25, hybrid retrieval, retrieval-augmented generation, hallucination, knowledge conflicts, corpus poisoning, out-of-domain retrieval, multi-hop verification, misinformation.
Feasibility and resourcesLexical indexes and small sentence encoders run on a laptop. The cost grows with large language models as verifiers: use a small model or a sample, and say so.
Track 3
Interventions and Causality
Measuring what changes in LLM behaviour

What is the causal effect of an intervention (a prompt, a tuning step, a model change) on a measurable aspect of a model’s behaviour?

Possible perspectives
  • Prompt level: persona, language, framing, wording, order of examples
  • Training level: base vs instruction-tuned, fine-tuning, model size
  • Inference level: quantisation, decoding, context length
  • Outcomes: sycophancy, accuracy, calibration, refusals, style, bias
  • Heterogeneous effects: where is the effect larger or reversed?
  • Prompt optimisation: does an optimised prompt generalise?
A good project has
  • A formalised design: treatment, control, outcome, unit, quantity to estimate
  • One change at a time: paired prompts, content controlled
  • A validly measured outcome: validated on a hand-annotated sample
  • Quantified uncertainty: intervals, paired tests, effect sizes; threats to validity
Common pitfalls
  • A few prompt variants, “one works better”, with no design or uncertainty
  • Conditions that differ in more than one respect
  • A causal effect on behaviour read as an explanation of the mechanism (Track 6)
Search keywordscausal inference in NLP, treatment effect, average treatment effect, instruction tuning, base vs instruct models, prompt sensitivity, persona prompting, sycophancy, calibration, quantisation effects, paired experiments, bootstrap confidence intervals, behavioural evaluation.
Feasibility and resourcesBase and instruction-tuned pairs of small open models and paired prompts on medium-size datasets run with little compute. The cost grows with models and repetitions: scale them to your resources.
Track 4
Culture and Language
Exploring the cultural sensitivity of LLMs

How do language models represent, recognise and reproduce cultural differences, and how faithful is that representation?

Possible perspectives
  • Values and norms: compare the answers with survey data
  • Factual representation: does it match statistical reality? cite primary sources
  • Stereotypes: associations, omissions, imbalances
  • Everyday knowledge: food, customs, festivities, etiquette, proverbs
  • Language and pragmatics: register, politeness, metaphor, style
  • Prompt language vs content: and variation within a culture
A good project has
  • A precise exploratory question: and what would contradict your expectation
  • An external reference for the culture: surveys, corpora, annotators, official statistics
  • At least two cultures or languages: with a motivation, and declared limits
  • Several formulations and samplings: structured qualitative analysis, with a codebook
Common pitfalls
  • Generalising from a handful of striking answers
  • Treating a country or a language as a homogeneous culture
  • An LLM that both produces and judges cultural content, with no human check
Search keywordscultural alignment, cultural bias, cross-cultural values, World Values Survey, Hofstede dimensions, Moral Foundations Theory, stereotypes in language models, multilingual evaluation, culturally-aware NLP, everyday knowledge benchmarks, representation and erasure, low-resource languages.
Feasibility and resourcesPrompts, benchmarks and public survey data work with small models, but you need reasonably good multilingual models: quality in under-represented languages is itself a result. Knowing languages other than English is an advantage.
Track 5
Literature and Language
LLMs as readers and as writers

What do language models understand when they read literary texts, and what do they do when they write them?

Possible perspectives
  • Models as readers: characters, plot, tone, irony, long texts, intertextuality
  • Memorisation vs reading: well-known and unknown works
  • Models as writers: diversity, style, structure, originality
  • Imitation: an author, a genre, a period
  • Constraints and control: prompts, constraints, tuning
  • Evaluation: literary quality, creativity, human judgement
A good project has
  • A human point of comparison: authors, readers, annotations, judgements
  • A justified and validated measure: checked on a sample against human judgement
  • Control of memorisation: recent, little-known or unpublished texts
  • Systematic reading and rigour: explicit criteria, many samples
Common pitfalls
  • A few stylometric features and a classifier, with no question beyond “can we tell them apart?”
  • “Creativity” measured by a single unvalidated metric
  • Comprehension tested on canonical works the model has memorised
Search keywordscomputational literary studies, narrative understanding, story generation, stylometry, authorship attribution, creativity evaluation, text diversity, long-context comprehension, literary text analysis, narrative structure, character tracking, memorisation, machine-generated text.
Feasibility and resourcesPublic-domain texts and short generations are cheap to process, even with small models. Mind the context window and work on excerpts. Copyright: use public-domain works or short excerpts, and cite your sources.
Track 6
Inside the Model
Mechanistic interpretability of a phenomenon

How does a model implement a specific behaviour, and can we test our explanation with interventions on its internals?

Possible perspectives
  • Linguistic phenomena: agreement, coreference, negation, tense, number
  • Factual recall and relations: where does the model retrieve a fact?
  • Pattern copying and induction: in-context learning, repetition
  • Simple reasoning or arithmetic: which components compute?
  • Behavioural tendencies: sycophancy, refusal, sentiment
  • Representations: directions, subspaces, features: can they be manipulated?
A good project has
  • A measurable phenomenon: controlled inputs, e.g. a logit difference
  • A falsifiable hypothesis: stated in advance, with what would refute it
  • Causal evidence: ablation, patching or steering, not only probes
  • Controls and declared limits: random components, corrupted inputs, many prompts
Common pitfalls
  • Attention maps or probe accuracies presented as an explanation
  • A phenomenon with no clear, measurable outcome
  • A hypothesis verified on one single prompt template
Search keywordsmechanistic interpretability, circuits, activation patching, causal tracing, logit lens, probing, attribution, induction heads, sparse autoencoders, superposition, steering vectors, representation engineering, faithfulness of explanations.
Feasibility and resourcesSmall models (GPT-2 sized, Pythia, a few hundred million parameters) and libraries such as TransformerLens are enough. The cost grows quickly with larger models. Unlike Track 3, causality here is inside the model.
Track 7
Rules, States and Moves
LLMs and board games

Can language models understand, track and reason about a game described in natural language?

Possible perspectives
  • Rulebook comprehension: questions, ambiguities, inconsistencies
  • State tracking: over a whole match
  • Legal moves and quality of play: against reference engines or agents
  • Formalisation: from a rulebook to an executable description
  • Modified rules: counterfactual variants of known games
  • Game design: new games, playtesting in simulation
A good project has
  • An executable ground truth: a simulator or a rule validator
  • Control of memorisation: lesser-known games, variants with modified rules
  • Separate metrics: rules, state and strategic quality
  • Experimental rigour: many games or states, errors classified by type
Common pitfalls
  • Only famous games: memorisation mistaken for understanding
  • Legality or quality judged by reading a few transcripts
  • “Fun” or “balance” with no simulation or human assessment
Search keywordsgame rule understanding, state tracking, board game benchmarks, general game playing, game description language, Ludii, procedural content generation, game design with LLMs, counterfactual rules, world models, Othello, game simulators.
Feasibility and resourcesGame states are short texts and the simulator runs locally: small models are enough. The cost depends on the number of games and moves. Check the licence and availability of rulebooks and data. For interaction between players, see Track 1.
Track 8
Beyond Text
Multimodal models

What does a non-textual modality add to a model, and does the model actually use it?

Possible perspectives
  • Does it use the image or the sound? or linguistic shortcuts?
  • Conflicts between modalities: text and image disagree
  • Sensitivity to the input: resolution, cropping, noise, order
  • Cultural and social representation: see Track 4
  • Multimodal fact-checking: see Track 2
  • Documents and charts: tables, figures, layouts
  • Speech and audio: accents, languages, noise
  • Joint representations: where does the alignment fail?
A good project has
  • The contribution of the modality isolated: text-only baseline, ablations
  • A question about the modality: not only about the benchmark
  • Validated evaluation: not an unchecked LLM judgement
  • Declared data and licences: sources, licences, privacy
Common pitfalls
  • Benchmark accuracy of an off-the-shelf model, with no hypothesis
  • Visual understanding confused with language priors
  • Images of people or personal data, ignoring licences and privacy
Search keywordsvision-language models, multimodal LLMs, CLIP, image captioning, visual question answering, multimodal hallucination, modality conflict, language priors, document understanding, speech recognition, audio-language models, cross-modal alignment, multimodal robustness.
Feasibility and resourcesThe most computationally demanding track. Small models (CLIP or SigLIP, Whisper, vision-language models of a few billion parameters) and data subsets run on free-tier GPUs or modest hardware; the cost grows quickly with model size and data volume.
Track 9
Trust and Provenance
Prompt injection, watermarking and detection

How robust are the mechanisms that protect language models and identify what they generate?

Possible perspectives
  • Prompt injection: direct and indirect: documents, tools, agents
  • Watermarking: detectability, quality, robustness to paraphrase
  • Detection of generated text: reliability, languages, non-native writers
  • Memorisation and privacy: membership inference, extraction
  • Poisoning and backdoors: tampered training data or corpora
  • Safety and utility trade-offs: what does a defence cost?
A good project has
  • An explicit threat model: who attacks, what they know and can do
  • A safety and utility trade-off: attack success and loss on the legitimate task
  • Correct detection metrics: true positive rate at a low fixed false positive rate
  • An adaptive adversary, in a sandbox: public benchmarks, never real third-party systems
Common pitfalls
  • A defence evaluated only against attacks not designed against it
  • Detection accuracy on a balanced set, ignoring false positives on human text
  • A defence called robust without measuring the loss in utility
Search keywordsprompt injection, indirect prompt injection, jailbreaks, LLM agents security, watermarking language models, machine-generated text detection, paraphrase attacks, membership inference, training data extraction, data poisoning, backdoors, adaptive attacks, AI-generated text detection fairness.
Feasibility and resourcesWatermarking with small open models and injection experiments on public benchmarks are accessible. Ethics: sandboxed environments or public benchmarks only, no tests on real systems of third parties, no operational attack tools.
Track 10
Training on Generated Text
Synthetic data and model collapse

What happens when models learn from text produced by other models, and when does it help or harm?

Possible perspectives
  • Recursive training: over several generations
  • Real and synthetic data: proportions; replacement vs accumulation
  • What degrades: diversity, calibration, style, bias, minorities
  • Prevention: filtering and selection of synthetic data
  • Contaminated corpora: web corpora with generated text
  • Distillation, self-generated data: when does a stronger source help?
A good project has
  • A controlled and declared loop: generations, quantity, scheme
  • A real-data baseline at the same budget: to separate the effect of synthetic data
  • Metrics beyond perplexity: diversity, tails, divergence, task utility
  • Systematic variation and realism: proportions, seeds, uncertainty
Common pitfalls
  • Degradation shown in an unrealistic setting, with no discussion
  • Reporting only perplexity
  • A baseline that uses a different amount of data
Search keywordsmodel collapse, synthetic data, recursive training, self-consuming generative models, data contamination, distillation, self-instruct, diversity of generated text, tail distribution, data mixing, training on AI-generated data.
Feasibility and resourcesSmall models (GPT-2 sized or distilled, or small ones trained from scratch) and reduced datasets are enough. The cost grows with the number of generations: scale it to your resources and justify it.
Choosing a track

Some tracks are neighbours: know where each one ends.

1 · 7
Interactive strategy or rules and state. Track 1 studies strategic behaviour between players; Track 7 focuses on the rules and the state of the game, with an executable ground truth.
3 · 6
Behaviour or mechanism. Track 3 estimates causal effects on the behaviour of the model (input and output); Track 6 studies causality inside the model, in its components.
2 · 8
Multimodal fact-checking. Claims with images, charts or tables sit between the retrieval track and the multimodal one.
4 · 8
Culture in images and sound. How models depict or describe people, places and practices across cultures links cultural sensitivity and multimodal models.
2 · 9
Poisoning and backdoors. What happens when the training data or a retrieval corpus is tampered with is a theme of both Track 9 and Track 2.
